Where the uncertainty of a solar neutrino result comes from

A rate or a day-night asymmetry is built from five steps, each fixed by a SNUfish benchmark: the solar model (B1), the oscillations inside the Sun (B2), the crossing of the Earth (B3), the cross section (B4), and the folding into a rate (B5). Each step has its own spread: published uncertainties, a choice between models, or an approximation that some tools make. The table attributes every source of the calculator's error budget to the step it comes from, for five reference observables, counting state-of-the-art inputs only: published uncertainties and the spread between competing current models. Approximations and legacy models are shown on each benchmark's own page, never counted here.

Largest effect of each step, relative to the central value

Sampled: the 1σ spread from published uncertainties. Model range: the largest deviation from the nominal among the competing state-of-the-art alternatives (not a σ); legacy models are plotted and labelled on each step's page but never counted here. The oscillation parameters enter B2 and B3 and are shown on their own.

B1 · Standard solar models

Ten published standard solar models (B16, BS05, B23 with different surface compositions) in one format: the flux of every component with its uncertainty, the electron density ne(r) and where each component is produced. B1 sets the normalisation of every rate: the flux uncertainty (sampled, with the B23 correlations) and the spread between models (the composition problem) are the largest inputs to most rates. The density and production profiles enter through B2. Three of the ten are the state of the art: B23 with the MB22 abundances (meteoritic and photospheric scales; high metallicity, favoured by helioseismology and the Borexino CNO rate) and B23 with AAG21 (low metallicity). Their spread is the composition problem as it stands; the other seven are legacy, labelled so, and kept to reproduce published numbers.

B2 · Oscillations inside the Sun

For the measured parameters the propagation out of the Sun is adiabatic: a neutrino leaves as a mass eigenstate with probability |Vei|² set by the electron density where it was produced. Each component is produced in its own region (⁸B at 0.05 R☉, ⁷Be at 0.07, pp at 0.12, hep at 0.14), so each has its own Pee(E). SNUfish averages over each component's production profile. Solvers that put every component at one point, or on ⁸B's profile, shift the result; the shift is largest for hep, produced furthest out (Lopes & Turck-Chièze, arXiv:1302.2791; Zaidel & Beacom, arXiv:2504.10583).

B3 · Crossing the Earth

At night the mass eigenstates cross the Earth and partly regenerate νe: the day-night asymmetry. B3 compares P(νi → νe) through PREM in three levels of detail (ten regions, four regions, Prob3++'s four shells), averaged over each site's night. The state of the art is PREM with the local CRUST1.0 crust; it is the only current Earth model, so B3 has no model range yet (that needs a second one, e.g. a 3D Earth). The bars below are approximations, signed shifts against it: the simpler Earth models, and NuFast-Earth's mean density per region crossing (the engine's night) against thin shells. The Earth hardly moves a rate; the asymmetry it moves by up to tens of per cent when the crust is left out.

B4 · Cross sections

Elastic scattering on electrons is known to well under 1 %: radiative corrections (Bahcall, Kamionkowski & Sirlin) and SNOwGLoBES's tables differ by a per mille or less; tree level, an approximation, is 2–4 % high. νe CC on ⁴⁰Ar is not: the state-of-the-art MARLEY 2 calculations, one per set of measured Gamow–Teller strengths, differ by about 11 % for ⁸B above 5 MeV, and the strengths of each ⁴⁰K level give a sampled uncertainty of several per cent. The older tables (KLMV, SNOwGLoBES's, MARLEY v1) are legacy and differ by up to 37 %; they are plotted for reference, not counted. For argon detectors B4 is the largest uncertainty after the flux.

B5 · Interaction rates

B5 folds B1 fluxes, B2 oscillations and B4 cross sections into interactions per kiloton-year. It adds no input of its own, so no uncertainty in the table: what it tests is the folding. Tools that compute the whole chain (SNOwGLoBES) are compared with SNUfish's folding of exactly the same fluxes and cross sections.

Discrepancies · the calculator's result templates

Every result template of the calculator sets each step to a paper's inputs. Each one is computed here twice, to keep apart two kinds of divergence:

  • Reproduction: SNUfish with the paper's own assumptions (its solar model, oscillation parameters, cross section, Earth model) against the number the paper publishes. What remains is what SNUfish cannot reproduce: rounding, an undocumented choice of the paper, or a difference of method; each template's note says which.
  • Update: SNUfish again, with every legacy input and approximation replaced by the state of the art (which inputs are which), against SNUfish with the paper's. That is what newer models and measurements change in the prediction: not an error of the paper, and not an uncertainty. A paper's own oscillation parameters are kept: they are the scenario its number answers (an asymmetry at a stated Δm²).

For measurements the pull says how far each prediction lies from the measured value. Day-night asymmetries are in the papers' convention, (D − N)/mean. See also docs/crosschecks.md.
